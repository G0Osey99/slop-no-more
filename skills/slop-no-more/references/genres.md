# Genre routing

Find the row, keep what is fixed, and hunt the listed tells first. The genre changes the target register, not the contract.

## Academic: papers, reflections, doctrinal or position statements, discussion sections

**Keep:** the assignment's required structure, headings, labels, and section order; formula openings the format repeats ("Statement: I believe…"); citation style; technical terms; first person if the assignment uses it; any required "how strongly or provisionally" or "application" component (keep the content, express it naturally, not as one repeated label); required paragraph counts per section; quotations and references exactly.
**Top tells:** one template per section; the same stance frame repeated ("I hold firmly that…", "This shows that…") or a new label rotated in for each section ("I'm convinced", "my settled view", "I'm just as sure"); paired modifiers ("final and fullest"); every section closing on an application maxim; no sentence over ~30 words; summary sentences that say what a paragraph proved; "some scholars argue" with no names; definitions the professor doesn't need.
**Moves:** work inside each section rather than moving paragraphs between them (a required application stays where the assignment puts it); let the argument sentences run longer and looser where the reasoning is connected; vary how each stance is announced; put the real names (councils, theologians, texts) at the front of sentences; make the application specific to what the source says about the author's life or church; keep a dry admission if the draft has one.
**Register:** formal-plain. Contractions are fine in a reflection paper and rare in a formal paper; follow the draft. Never slang.

## Application and personal statements: college essays, scholarship, grad school

**Keep:** every fact about the applicant; the prompt's required elements; the word limit.
**Top tells:** a scene-setting opener and a "this taught me" ending; an internal-realization arc; triplets of virtues ("resilience, empathy, and leadership"); "I learned the importance of…"; a mission-statement closing paragraph; an extended metaphor (a bridge, a compass, two worlds).
**Moves:** re-voice in the applicant's own register (a 17-year-old writing carefully, with contractions); open on a concrete moment from the source; replace the stated lesson with what the applicant did next as the source gives it; cut the metaphor scaffolding; keep the specific nouns (the dish, the street, the grandmother's phrase); keep the paragraph breaks. Don't invent new anecdotes or dialogue. If the essay needs a detail it doesn't have, ask the user.

## Cover letters

**Keep:** employer, role, dates, skills, tools, and accomplishments exactly as given.
**Top tells:** "I am excited to apply…" openers; "I am confident that my skills…"; a three-adjective self-description; "aligns with your mission"; restating the job ad; "I look forward to the opportunity to discuss".
**Moves:** write in the applicant's own voice, so a new grad sounds like a capable new grad, with natural contractions and no "I am writing to express". Lead with the most relevant concrete thing (the dashboard, the dataset, the result) and give one sentence on why this employer, using only facts in the source. Close plainly. Don't cut a claim just because it sounds generic. Rewrite it so it says something.

## Professional email and memos

**Keep:** dates, times, owners, decisions, links, numbers.
**Top tells:** "I hope this email finds you well"; bold-labeled bullet sections for a short message; "I'm excited to share"; a rationale paragraph before the decision; "please don't hesitate to reach out"; a recap of what the reader already knows.
**Moves:** decision first, then what changes for the reader, then the reason in one or two sentences. Keep bullets for real lists (dates, steps, separate instructions) and drop the bold labels. Write it the way the sender talks to this team ("I do want to hear them", "Don't hold it for a meeting"). Sign off like a colleague.

## Social and blog posts (LinkedIn, Substack, Medium)

**Keep:** the facts, numbers, and opinions.
**Top tells:** one-sentence paragraphs stacked down the page; "Here's what I learned:" followed by numbered lessons; not-X-but-Y contrasts; "Read that again."; ending on a question to drive engagement ("What would you do?"); emoji bullets.
**Moves:** merge one-line paragraphs where they make one point; tell what happened in order with the actual numbers; rewrite the lead-in lines ("Here's what I learned:") in the author's voice. Keep a numbered list of takeaways if the author wrote one: it's the genre's convention and readers scan it. Keep a closing question or call to action the author wrote (a reworded one is fine, a dropped one is content loss).

## Technical docs, READMEs, PR descriptions, postmortems

**Keep:** code, commands, paths, versions, error text verbatim.
**Top tells:** a heading for every two sentences; "This comprehensive guide…"; feature lists with bold labels; "seamlessly", "robust", "powerful"; narrating what the next line of code does; a closing summary.
**Moves:** say what it does, how to run it, and what can go wrong. A postmortem names the actual mistake. Use as few headings as possible.

## Speeches, sermons, devotionals

**Keep:** Scripture references and quotations exactly; the speaker's theological positions.
**Top tells:** a triadic crescendo in every paragraph; "Friends, …" openers; lyrical abstraction ("a love that never fails, never fades, never falters"); each point ending in a quotable line.
**Moves:** spoken syntax is looser than written, so let sentences run and use contractions. Keep one strong refrain if the speaker uses it, and cut the rest.
